Galápagos Whale Shark Project · Field partner

The largest fish in the ocean, and we still know almost nothing about how it reproduces.

Almost every whale shark recorded at Darwin Island is a large adult female, most of them apparently pregnant. Nowhere else on earth does that pattern hold. Since 2019 we have funded satellite tags and tracking time, contributed photo identifications to Sharkbook.ai, and carried a project scientist on every Citizen Science departure so the work continues season after season.

Our mission is to preserve marine ecosystems through research and education, to make science accessible to divers of all levels, and to raise awareness of endangered marine species. Our vision is a fully protected marine ecosystem in Galápagos, and a protected marine corridor to Cocos Island.

5,000kilometres tracked

The furthest-travelling whale shark the project has tracked is Elise, who carried her tag roughly five thousand kilometres out into the Pacific before transmission ended. She is identifiable by a large notch on her dorsal fin and a missing tip on her left pectoral fin.

What the season did not resolve. Ultrasound imaging still struggles to penetrate the thick skin of an adult whale shark, and adverse weather cost working days at Darwin. Reproductive imaging of this species was first attempted here in 2023; it is an open line of research, not a settled result.

Sources: Galápagos Whale Shark Project 2024 field-season report, published by Galapagos Shark Diving; Name a Whale Shark results 2022–2025. First ultrasound work reported by Forbes, 23 March 2023.

Method

How citizen science actually works here.

Guest contribution and scientist-led work are deliberately separated. Divers generate identification data under a standard protocol; the project’s scientists do the tagging, the ultrasound and the sampling. Nothing a guest submits is treated as a result until it has been reviewed.

01

Capture to protocol

Photograph the spot pattern behind the gills, left or right side, as square to the animal as possible. Cameras are set to local time before the first dive so every frame carries a usable timestamp.

02

Standardised counts

Observations are logged with the Shark Count protocol, which covers the top twenty dive sites in the archipelago. All of that data is shared with the Galápagos National Park Directorate.

03

Review, then submission

Frames are reviewed on board with the scientist aboard before submission to Sharkbook.ai, where pattern matching identifies whether the animal is already on the register or new to it.

04

Scientist-led fieldwork

Satellite tagging, in-water ultrasound and blood sampling are carried out only by Galápagos Whale Shark Project scientists, under their own permits and protocols.
