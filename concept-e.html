The Seamount HallsDarwin and Wolf, and the animals that gather there

Two small islands stand at the northern edge of the archipelago, where the archipelago's shelf drops away into the deep Pacific. Almost nobody lives within a day of them. The galleries that follow set out what is known about the animals that gather at Darwin and Wolf, how that knowledge is gathered, and the two vessels that carry sixteen guests at a time to see it.

Gallery I

Darwin Island

Darwin is the northernmost island of the Galápagos and the reason most divers come. Its arch collapsed in part in 2021; the pillars that remain sit above a plateau where scalloped hammerheads school and, between June and November, adult whale sharks pass through. The published eight-day itinerary spends four dives at Darwin's Arch on the first day there and two more on the second.

Didactic panel · I.a

Why the adults come here

Most whale sharks recorded around the world are young males. At Darwin the pattern inverts: the animals seen here are overwhelmingly large adult females. Since 2019 the Galápagos Whale Shark Project has placed a shark scientist aboard every Citizen Science departure, and from 2025 the project has been using ultrasound to investigate whether those females are pregnant. That question is open. The exhibition records it as an open question.

Gallery II

Wolf Island

Wolf lies south of Darwin and carries the higher shark biomass of the two. Current is the defining condition: divers hook in and watch, rather than swim. The eight-day itinerary gives Wolf two dives on one afternoon and three the following day; the fifteen-day itinerary returns a third time and adds a night dive. Guests on Wolf and Darwin dives have recorded schooling hammerheads in the hundreds.

Didactic panel · II.a

What the circuit covers besides the seamounts

Darwin and Wolf are the reason for the voyage, but not the whole of it. The published eight-day route also dives Cape Marshall and City of Mantas on Isabela, Punta Vicente Roca, Cape Douglas on Fernandina, and Cousin's Rock off Santiago, closing with a land excursion into the Santa Cruz highlands.

The fifteen-day variant extends time at Wolf and Darwin and adds Roca Blanca, Pijón, Beagle Rocks, Marchena and Roca Redonda. Nothing here is promised: these are wild animals in open ocean, and the record notes what was seen rather than what was advertised.

Gallery III

The Research Wing

Galapagos Shark Diving has supported the Galápagos Whale Shark Project since 2019. The relationship is practical rather than decorative: a project scientist sails on every Citizen Science departure, and guest photographs may enter an international photo-identification record — but only after review.

Process case · III.a

How a photograph becomes a record

  1. 01ObserveA scientist aboard explains what makes an identification photograph usable, and what disturbs the animal.
  2. 02PhotographEach whale shark carries a unique pattern of spots behind the gills. Photographed from the correct angle, that pattern identifies the individual.
  3. 03SubmitSuitable frames are submitted. Guests also contribute standardised observation counts, including the SharkCount protocol, and debris data.
  4. 04ReviewImages are reviewed before they are accepted. Accepted contributions go into Sharkbook.ai, the international photo-identification database.

Methods on the project side, which are scientist-led rather than guest-led: satellite tagging, blood sampling, in-water behavioural research, and since 2025 ultrasound.
